J. J. Engola — Capillary Dynamics

A progressive analytical and computational research-training program connecting fundamental dynamical models to research-level problems in transient capillary transport.

Research Training Profile

Ph.D. Researcher: J. J. Engola

Research Supervisor: Prof. Martin N. Azese

Research Area: Unsteady capillary transport in rectangular and circular conduits, including gravity, evolving surface tension, entrance effects, and transient meniscus dynamics.

This series documents selected analytical and computational projects undertaken as part of J. J. Engola's doctoral research training. The projects are designed to develop progressively the mathematical, computational, physical, and critical-analysis skills required for independent research in fluid mechanics.

Rather than presenting only final research outcomes, this platform makes selected stages of the training process accessible. Each project emphasizes derivation, verification, computation, visualization, physical interpretation, and assessment of model limitations.

Prior Research Foundation

Engola's present doctoral research builds upon an earlier research collaboration in capillary fluid dynamics. As a Master's student, he contributed as a co-author to research published in the Journal of Fluid Mechanics.

M. N. Azese, J. J. Engola, J. Hona, E. J. Yap & Y. C. Mbono Samba (2022).
Examining capillary dynamics in rectangular and circular conduits subject to unsteady surface tension.
Journal of Fluid Mechanics, 949, A6.

This earlier work provides part of the scientific foundation for Engola's present Ph.D. research, which extends the investigation of transient capillary transport to incorporate gravity and entrance effects, together with the resulting richer meniscus dynamics.

Connection to the Ph.D. Research

The training modules provide analytical and computational bridges toward a broader investigation of transient capillary dynamics.

The doctoral research extends the study of capillary transport in rectangular and circular conduits subject to unsteady surface tension by incorporating gravity and entrance effects. The research examines how capillary forcing, gravitational resistance, viscous dissipation, liquid-column inertia, conduit geometry, and entrance dynamics interact to produce cessation, overshoot, reversal, rebound, and oscillatory relaxation.

The progressive training exercises therefore serve not as substitutes for the complete nonlinear model, but as controlled environments in which individual mathematical and physical mechanisms can be isolated, understood, tested, and subsequently connected to the full research problem.
